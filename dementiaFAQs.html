Terminology

What is dementia?
Dementia is a thinking problem that limits day-to-day activities (e.g., shopping, housekeeping, accounting, food preparation, or transportation). The spectrum of thinking problems includes subjective cognitive decline, minor neurocognitive disorder (mild cognitive impairment), and major neurocognitive disorder (dementia).

Subjective cognitive decline
- Memory loss per patient or loved one
- Normal memory test
- No need for help		 Minor neurocognitive disorder
- Memory loss per patient or loved one
- Abnormal memory test
- No need for help		 Major neurocognitive disorder
- Memory loss per patient or loved one
- Abnormal memory test
- Dependent on others
         

Is Alzheimer disease the same as dementia?
No, Alzheimer disease is NOT the same as dementia: dementia refers to symptoms, Alzheimer disease refers to cause. Dementia is what you see in the room, Alzheimer disease is what you see under the microscope. Patients with Alzheimer disease develop dementia, however, not all patients with dementia have Alzheimer disease, there are many other causes.

What is Alzheimer disease?
Alzheimer disease refers to amyloid plaques, also tau tangles. Amyloid plaques and tau tangles are protein aggregates only visible with a microscope.

Amyloid is unique to Alzheimer disease, however, tau has a better correlate with symptoms:
- memory centers --> typical Alzheimer disease
- vision centers --> visual variant Alzheimer disease (i.e., posterior cortical atrophy)
- language centers --> language variant Alzheimer disease (i.e., logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia)
- decision-making centers --> behavioral variant Alzheimer disease

What are other causes of dementia?
Other causes of dementia include neuro-degeneration (e.g., Lewy-body disease or frontotemporal dementia), metabolic disease (e.g., thyroid disease, cirrhosis, or kidney failure), toxins (e.g., alcohol), deficiencies (e.g., B12 or folate), trauma (e.g., chronic traumatic encephalopathy), and inflammation (e.g., autoimmune encephalitis or central nervous system vasculitis).

Anti-amyloid medications for Alzheimer disease

What are anti-amyloid medications?
Anti-amyloid medications are disease-modifying medications that target amyloid and slow progression of Alzheimer disease by 27-35 % (months to years). They're only effective for Alzheimer disease because amyloid is unique to Alzheimer disease.

The 2 currently-approved anti-amyloid medications are:
- Leqembi (lecanemab) [IV every 2 weeks]
- Kisunla (donanemab) [IV every 4 weeks]

Leqembi (lecanemab) * FDA-approved as of 01/06/23
Slows disease by 27 %
Prevents new amyloid plaques from forming
[10 mg/kg IV every 2 weeks], after 18 months --> [10 mg/kg IV or subcutaneous every 2-4 weeks]
Lower risk for edema and hemorrhage vs donanemab
MRI of the head prior to infusion #'s 3, 5, 7, 14, and 26
Stop if progression or complications

Kisunla (donanemab) * FDA-approved as of 07/02/24
Slows disease by 35 %
Gets rid of existing amyloid plaques
[IV every 4 weeks, 350 mg # 1, 700 # 2, 1,050 # 3, 1,400 all other times]
Higher risk for edema and hemorrhage vs lecanemab
MRI of the head prior to infusion #'s 2, 3, 4, and 7
Amyloid scan of the head [every 6 months]
Stop if progression, complications, or negative amyloid scan of the head

Why choose one anti-amyloid medication over another?
Leqembi is safer (0.4-1.4 % risk of serious complications) but less effective (27 % vs 35) and more inconvenient (every 2 weeks vs 4). Kisunla is more effective (35 % vs 27) and more convenient (every 4 weeks vs 2) but less safe (2-3 times the risk of serious complications). An 8 % difference over 4 years is 4 months.

How are serious complications prevented?
Most patients develop mild complications before serious, identifiable on MRI of the head.

The protocol for Leqembi (lecanemab) is MRI's prior to infusion #'s 3, 5, 7, 14, and 26.

The protocol for Kisunla (donanemab) is MRI's prior to infusion #'s 2, 3, 4, and 7.

If mild abnormalities on MRI but no symptoms, no need to stop treatment. If moderate abnormalities on MRI or mild-to-moderate symptoms, hold treatment. If severe abnormalities on MRI or severe symptoms, stop treatment.

Who qualifies for anti-amyloid medications?
Patients that meet all of the following criteria qualify for anti-amyloid medications:
- minor neuro-cognitive disorder or mild dementia
- Alzheimer biomarker (positive amyloid scan of the head, lumbar puncture, or blood test)
- MoCA score of ≥ 16 for Leqembi (lecanemab) or 13 for Kisunla (donanemab)

Who doesn't qualify for anti-amyloid medications?
Patients with any of the following do NOT qualify for anti-amyloid medications:
- 5 micro-bleeds, 1 surface bleed, or 1 pocket of blood
- anticoagulation (e.g., Coumadin, Eliquis, Xarelto, or Lovenox) * aspirin, Plavix okay
- weak blood vessels in the brain (e.g., aneurysm)
- severe cholesterol build-up
- MRI limitations: open scanner or sedation
- 2 E4 alleles

When does treatment stop?
There are 2 stopping points for Leqembi (lecanemab):
- progression (i.e., no longer mild)
- complications

There are 3 stopping points for Kisunla (donanemab):
- progression (i.e., no longer mild)
- complications
- negative amyloid scan of the head

What needs to happen before anti-amyloid medications?
1.) First appointment: test memory
2.) Check for non-Alzheimer causes of dementia with MRI of the head
3.) Prove Alzheimer disease with Alzheimer biomarker (amyloid scan of the head, lumbar puncture, or blood test)
4.) Determine risk with apolipoprotein E genotype (blood work) * after insurance authorization
5.) Second appointment: test memory again, review risks, and sign paperwork
6.) Schedule treatments, also images of the head * after insurance authorizion
7.) Follow-up appointments [every 6 months]: test memory again
